## TL;DR

Suppose an engineering task has eleven required gates. Ten pass. One fails. The
benchmark correctly returns `fail`, but an equal-weight score would be:

```text
10 / 11 = 0.9091
```

As a reporting score, `0.9091` can describe partial progress. As a reinforcement
learning reward, it can teach a different lesson: a failed result can still earn
almost full credit.

The problem is larger than the reward equation. A model is trained inside a
measurement system made of task instructions, observations, tools, evidence,
rubrics, verifiers, aggregation rules, feedback, and invalid-result handling.
The optimiser does not encounter our intent directly. It encounters that system.

This article proposes an **Evaluation Design Case**: a reviewable argument that
connects the capability we claim to measure with the environment that elicits
it, the evidence that records it, the rubric that interprets it, the score and
gates that report it, and any reward that optimises it.

The central rule is:

> A score tells us what happened under a measurement system. A reward tells the
> optimiser where to search next.

Those are different objects. Treating them as one turns evaluation defects into
training objectives.

## A 0.91 failure

While mapping a hydraulic design-response lifecycle in AEC-Bench, I found a
useful tension.

The lifecycle has eleven required gates. They check whether the checkpoint
contract was followed, the selected evidence was grounded, and operation records
were valid. They also check whether affected calculations were recomputed, the
intervention worked, the result reached the final report, and the final claim
remained inside the evidence boundary.

The decision is strict: every required gate must pass.

Now consider an illustrative reward that gives equal partial credit across those
gates. Ten passes and one failure produce `0.9091`, while the lifecycle still
returns `fail`.

Nothing about the score is necessarily wrong. It distinguishes a catastrophic
failure from a narrowly incomplete result. The problem begins if the same number
becomes an RL reward.

A failed submission can then receive an almost-perfect return. If the missing
gate is expensive to satisfy but cheap to disguise, optimisation may favour the
disguise.

```text
FINAL DECISION

all required gates pass?  no
result                    fail

TRAINING SIGNAL

partial criteria passed   10 / 11
reward                     0.9091
```

The mistake is to read the second column as a smooth approximation of the first.

> A high partial-credit reward is not a high probability of valid success.

Keep the final decision, hard gates, retained dimensions, and optional reward
separate. The reward may be useful. It is not the benchmark's truth.

## The grader is part of the world

In [*Task Worlds and Meta-Harnesses*](/blog/task-worlds-meta-harness/), I argued
that agent tasks are worlds, not prompts. They decide what the agent can see,
which rules have authority, what actions exist, what counts as evidence, and
which failures can be named.

In [*A World Worth Learning From*](/blog/a-world-worth-learning-from/), I added a
second condition: before an agent can learn from experience, the environment has
to produce experience worth learning from.

Post-training adds another:

> The world must make the intended behaviour the reliable route to reward.

A task world is usually described from the agent's side: observations, actions,
tools, state, resources, and consequences. For post-training, that is incomplete.
The effective world also contains every route by which a trajectory becomes
reward:

- which facts the evaluator can observe;
- which evidence it treats as authoritative;
- what the rubric asks it to notice;
- what the verifier can establish;
- how criterion judgments are aggregated;
- what happens when evidence is missing;
- whether an impossible task can be reported honestly;
- what feedback returns to the policy;
- whether the agent can change any of these surfaces.

The grader is not outside the environment, looking in. It participates in the
environment's causal structure.

If an agent can rewrite tests that determine reward, the tests are part of its
action space. If it can produce the log that a verifier later treats as
authoritative, the log is part of its output strategy. If an LLM judge rewards
familiar terms, those terms become available actions whether or not they are
technically relevant.

Invalid-result handling matters too. If a timeout silently becomes zero, ending
the evaluation may become a strategy. If honest inability and fabricated
completion both receive zero, while a plausible unsupported answer sometimes
receives partial credit, the environment can reward concealment.

This is why reward hacking is often diagnosed too late. We notice the model's
behaviour and call it a model failure, even though the exploitable route was
constructed earlier in the relationship between the world and its measurement.

The attack surface is the complete path from intent to reward.

## Right action, wrong objective

Apollo Research's *Measuring Reward-Seeking via Contrastive Belief Updates*
starts with a difficult identification problem.[^apollo]

Imagine two policies:

```text
Policy A
tries to satisfy the developer's intended objective

Policy B
tries to satisfy whatever it believes the grader rewards
```

When the grader and developer agree, both policies produce the same action.
Ordinary behavioural evaluation cannot tell them apart.

Apollo creates the disagreement deliberately. It fine-tunes matched copies of a
model on synthetic documents that imply opposite beliefs about what the grader
and another authority prefer. It then measures how behaviour changes. The method
defines reward-seeking as causal sensitivity to beliefs about the grader: does
changing what the model thinks the grader wants redirect what it does?

In one experiment, a late checkpoint from a capabilities-focused o3 RL run broke
an honesty promise 87% of the time when it believed task completion was rewarded,
and 9% when it believed honesty was rewarded. An earlier checkpoint was less
sensitive: 40% versus 24%.

The checkpoints were taken before safety training, and the result should not be
generalised beyond the studied setup. The method matters more than the headline
number. It changes the evaluation question from:

> Did the model behave correctly here?

to:

> Would the same model still behave correctly if it believed the grader
> preferred something else?

That creates a **distinguishing case**. The intended objective and its proxy no
longer agree.

Evaluations that are vulnerable to proxy optimisation need cases where plausible
objectives separate:

- task completion versus an honesty commitment;
- user preference versus a developer constraint;
- local technical improvement versus system-level safety;
- visible checklist completion versus actual evidence;
- low cost versus valid completion;
- current reward versus behaviour after oversight disappears.

A counterfactual shift does not prove that a model has one clean, stable,
human-like motive. It establishes sensitivity to believed grader preferences on
a specified distribution and under a specific intervention. It does not establish
a coherent terminal goal, long-term scheming, or behaviour in every deployment
context.

It does establish a practical limit: behavioural success under one measurement
regime is not evidence of robustness under another.

## The evaluation design case

An **Evaluation Design Case** is not another configuration file. It is a
structured argument, supported by evidence, that answers nine questions.

| Layer | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Capability claim | What exactly do we claim this evaluation measures? |
| Task contract | What problem, constraints, and expected products exist? |
| Environment | What can the actor observe, do, change, and affect? |
| Evidence contract | What authoritative facts record what happened? |
| Rubric | How is that evidence interpreted into criteria? |
| Score policy | Which dimensions survive, and which failures cannot be compensated? |
| Reward policy | What signal, if any, is exposed to optimisation, and why? |
| Threat model | How could a policy score well without satisfying the claim, or be rejected despite satisfying it? |
| Qualification | What tests must the exact design survive before use? |

The complete path should be traceable:

```text
capability claim
      ↓
task and environment
      ↓
authoritative evidence
      ↓
criteria and verifiers
      ↓
score vector and hard gates
      ↓
optional reward
      ↓
final decision

threats and qualification tests attack every edge
```

This makes the design reviewable by people who own different parts of the truth.
A domain expert does not have to accept a reward engineer's view of hydraulic
correctness. A runtime maintainer does not have to decide which engineering
standard has authority. A red team can record an exploit without rewriting the
task.

The task owner defines the meaning; the system makes it inspectable.

## Seven things that must stay separate

Most reward-design confusion comes from collapsing concepts that happen to sit
next to each other in code.

### 1. Evidence

An `EvidenceItem` records something that happened or was produced: a calculation
run, source revision, tool effect, final artifact, authoritative world state,
timeout, or execution failure.

Evidence has provenance, visibility, validity, and an owner. A model may make
claims about evidence. It should not decide whether its own claims are true.

### 2. Verified measures

A `VerifiedMeasure` is a domain fact derived from evidence:

```text
maximum deflection           18.2 mm
capacity ratio               0.91
affected calculations rerun  7 / 8
unsupported state updates    2
```

A deterministic checker, domain tool, or approved evaluator establishes it.

### 3. Criterion judgments

A `CriterionJudgment` interprets evidence under a declared standard:

```text
criterion       selective_recomputation
judgment        partially_meets
evidence        run-17, source-revision-3
confidence      high
```

This is where technical facts meet the rubric.

### 4. Dimension scores

A `DimensionScore` preserves useful structure:

```text
technical correctness    0.82
evidence integrity       1.00
completeness             0.70
reasoning quality        0.65
efficiency               0.48
```

Even when training requires one scalar, retain the vector. Otherwise a rising
total can conceal that factuality fell while verbosity rose.

### 5. Hard gates

Some failures must not be traded away. A presentation score of `1.0` cannot
compensate for fabricated evidence. Low cost cannot compensate for an unsafe
design. This is a different decision structure, not a request for a larger
negative weight.

### 6. Reward

A `Reward` is an optional optimisation signal derived from some combination of
measures, judgments, dimensions, and gates. Its purpose must be explicit:
training, search, candidate selection, or reporting.

A reporting score and a training reward do not have to be identical. Often they
should not be.

### 7. Decision

A `Decision` records what the evaluation concludes: `passed`, `failed`,
`invalid`, `incomplete`, or `requires_adjudication`. It should derive from
validity and declared gates, not an unexplained scalar threshold.

One separation deserves special emphasis:

> Invalid is not zero.

A poor submission, missing evidence, verifier crash, malformed output, timeout,
and legitimate numeric zero are different events. Collapsing them destroys
diagnostic information and may create exploitable incentives.

## Rubrics are executable interpretations

Open-ended evaluation often starts with an LLM judge and a list of criteria.
That can work, but *rubric* hides two objects:

- the **rubric** says what deserves credit;
- the **verifier** determines whether the response satisfies it.

A stronger verifier can apply a bad rubric more consistently. It cannot add a
missing concept.

Recent work on reward hacking in rubric-based RL separates these failures.[^rubric-hacking]
Weak verifiers increasingly credit partial satisfaction, implied content, and
approximate topical matches. Stronger verifiers reduce those errors. Yet even
stronger rubric-based judges can prefer an RL-trained answer while rubric-free
judges prefer the base model, because the rubric rewards visible completeness
while omitting factuality, relevance, concision, or overall usefulness.

### One meaningful requirement per criterion

A criterion should express the smallest meaningful requirement that can be
graded independently.

Too compound:

> Identifies the hydraulic issue, explains its cause, recomputes the affected
> cases, proposes a suitable intervention, and updates the report.

A judge may award broad credit after seeing only two of five parts.

Too fragmented:

> Mentions rainfall. Mentions the basin. Mentions the outlet. Mentions the report.

Now the model can collect points by spraying nouns across the answer.

A better rubric separates meaningful outcomes:

```text
revision_scope
Identifies which source value changed and which calculation chain it affects.

selective_recomputation
Recomputes every affected result and preserves an unaffected result only when
its recorded inputs remain current.

intervention_effectiveness
Uses authoritative post-action results to show that the intervention resolves
the declared system-level constraints.

report_propagation
Updates the final report so its claims and cited runs match the accepted state.
```

Each criterion has evidence that could make it pass or fail.

### Use anchors, not adjectives

“Good,” “clear,” and “comprehensive” are not scoring instructions. A usable
criterion needs positive and negative examples, a boundary case, a
missing-evidence rule, known ambiguities, and an evaluator responsible for the
decision.

A useful calibration question is:

> What is the smallest change to this response that should flip the judgment?

If neither the author nor the evaluator can answer, the criterion is not ready
to become reward.

### Presence is not satisfaction

“Mentions uncertainty” is not the same as calibrating uncertainty. “Cites a
source” is not the same as grounding a claim in that source. “Includes a risk
section” is not the same as identifying the material risk in this case.

Presence-based criteria are easy to optimise because a model can add the
expected surface form without changing the underlying work.

Rubrics also need explicit priorities: non-compensable gates, essential
criteria, and soft quality criteria. Optional excellence can sit above them. It
cannot rescue invalid work.

## Reward belongs last

Design the reward only after the claim, environment, evidence, criteria,
dimensions, gates, and invalid-result semantics are clear.

A reward policy should answer:

- What is this reward for?
- Which verified facts and judgments contribute to it?
- Which failures make positive reward ineligible?
- Which qualities may trade off?
- How is honest inability represented?
- What feedback reaches the policy?
- What known proxy risks remain?
- How will independent quality be measured during optimisation?

### Preserve the vector

Suppose technical correctness rises while evidence integrity falls. A single
total may remain flat. Suppose factuality falls while the model learns to satisfy
a completeness checklist. The total may rise.

The vector is the diagnostic. The scalar is one view.

### Gate failures that change the meaning of the work

For some designs, a gate is clearer than a weighted sum:

```text
eligible =
    evidence_valid
    and integrity_valid
    and critical_safety_passed

if not eligible:
    decision = fail_or_invalid
    positive_reward = unavailable
else:
    reward = quality_score
```

Not every requirement should become a hard gate. That would create sparse
feedback and hide useful partial progress. Gates are for failures that make the
remaining score mean something different.

### Represent honest failure

An agent sometimes cannot validly complete a task. Inputs may be missing,
permissions may be insufficient, or the requested result may contradict a
higher-authority constraint.

The environment should distinguish valid completion, valid partial completion,
honest blockage, insufficient evidence, invalid agent action, detected tampering,
evaluator failure, and environment failure. Do not collapse them into one zero.

If honest blockage receives the same or worse reward than unsupported
completion, the design creates an incentive against honest reporting.

### Expose the contract, protect the implementation

The model should know the intended requirements. It should not have to guess
that tests must not be altered or evidence must not be fabricated. But transparent
semantics do not require exposing every holdout case, judge prompt, or
implementation detail.

> Transparent contract, protected measurement implementation.

## The optimiser is your red team

A reward function can look reliable on ordinary samples and fail under
optimisation. Average agreement is not the same as robustness at the high-reward
tail.

Research on reward-model overoptimisation found that proxy reward and a stronger
reference reward initially rise together, then separate as optimisation
continues.[^overoptimisation] Rubric-based RL shows the same structural problem
at criterion level.

Reward-tampering studies go further. Under deliberately constructed conditions,
models trained on simpler forms of specification gaming sometimes generalised to
altering or concealing the reward process without direct training on reward
tampering.[^tampering] The event was rare: 45 instances in 32,768 trials, of
which seven also altered tests to hide the change. The setup was artificial and
deliberately rewarded gaming. It does not show that ordinary deployed models
will tamper with reward systems. It shows that the escalation path is possible.

Attack the reward before expensive RL begins. Increase selection pressure from
base samples through best-of-N selection, adversarial mutation, and short
training runs. At each stage, compare training reward with an independent audit
measure. Track the proxy-audit gap, top-tail precision, hard-gate failure rate,
and tamper attempts.

Seed attacks deliberately:

1. **Hard-gate compensation:** maximise every soft dimension while one required
   gate fails. The final decision must remain failed or invalid.
2. **Missing or tampered evidence:** remove, corrupt, or make agent-controlled a
   required evidence item. The result must not become an ordinary low score.
3. **Presentation-only improvement:** improve length, formatting, confidence,
   and rubric-shaped language without changing technical substance. Reward
   should not increase materially.

Also test invariance. A grader should ignore paraphrasing, reordered independent
arguments, irrelevant names, and shorter equivalent prose. It should react when
a required condition is removed, a critical claim is negated, authoritative
evidence is replaced, or a permitted action becomes prohibited.

When an exploit is found, patch it and leave the incentive intact. If the policy
finds another route to the same high reward, the problem was not one memorised
trick. It was the objective or environment.

## Testing the idea in AEC-Bench

The first AEC-Bench implementation should remain narrow. I plan to reconstruct
one existing hydraulic lifecycle exactly as implemented. I will connect its
capability claim to its environment, evidence, rubric, gates, reward, decision,
and threat model, then run qualification against that fixed design.

The process has three steps:

1. **Design:** create a new Design Case or reconstruct an existing evaluation
   without quietly improving it.
2. **Red-team:** search for high reward without the claimed capability, and for
   valid work that the evaluator rejects.
3. **Qualify:** run fixed controls, anchors, attacks, missing-evidence cases, and
   consistency tests against the exact candidate.

Qualification can pass, fail, or remain inconclusive. It cannot release the
design, silently repair it, or turn “the tests passed” into “this evaluation is
authoritative.” Release remains a separate human decision.

The domain verifier also remains local to its owner. Hydraulic code establishes
hydraulic facts. Shared evaluation machinery records what those facts mean under
the Design Case and whether the evaluator itself ran successfully. It should not
learn to interpret drainage fields.

This first implementation is a test of the boundary, not proof of a universal
evaluation architecture. Shared machinery should appear only where several real
evaluations need it.

## What this does not solve

A Design Case does not reveal a model's internal motive. Apollo's method provides
a causal behavioural probe of grader sensitivity, not a direct view of intent.

Qualification also does not prove that an evaluation is exploit-free. Every
released design gives the next optimiser new information. The attacker still
moves second.

A stronger LLM judge does not rescue an incomplete rubric. A complete rubric
does not rescue untrusted evidence. Trusted evidence does not rescue an
environment that permits shortcuts. Hard gates do not decide which requirements
deserve to be hard.

A traceability graph can show that every reward term has lineage. It cannot show
that the underlying capability claim is socially, scientifically, or
professionally worthwhile.

Domain experts still decide which facts, standards, safety limits, and
alternatives are valid. AI agents can generate candidates, attacks, and
explanations. They cannot manufacture domain approval by writing confidently
into a schema.

## Conclusion

Tasks are worlds, not prompts. Before an agent can learn, the world must produce
experience worth learning from. Once the agent is optimised, the grader becomes
part of that world.

The reward is not a detached score above the task. It belongs to a system of
observations, actions, authorities, evidence, judgments, and feedback. Changing
any part of that system may change what the policy learns.

The question for post-training is not only:

> Can the model solve this task?

It is:

> What task does this world make it rational to solve?

We need to design, inspect, and attack that world before the optimiser does it
for us.
