I was recently at a dinner with digital transformation and AI leaders from some of Australia’s largest organisations. In my introduction, I warned the room that I planned to be provocative. When AI governance came up, I kept the promise: I said that much of the governance now being built was designed to succeed on paper and fail in operation.

I gave two reasons. First, many AI governance structures are existing structures with AI added to the title. They inherit the same committees, approval paths, and decision cycles. Those structures were already slow. Renaming them does not make one decision cycle fit every AI use case.

Second, many of the people closest to current AI practice do not have a seat where the decisions are made. If they have a seat, they do not always have a voice. The people with formal authority can therefore be asked to make decisions without enough current deployment experience at the point where the trade-offs are made.

This is a systems problem, not a criticism of the people inside the system. Give capable people responsibility without access to current evidence and practitioners, or without the authority to act on what they learn, and the structure has already decided much of the result.

> A Chief AI Officer is not an AI capability.

A strong Chief AI Officer gives applied expertise a path to executive authority and clears barriers practitioners cannot clear alone.

## Governance at yesterday’s speed

In [*Mediation, Not Intermediation*](/blog/mediation-not-intermediation/), I argued that governance should be informed by practice, designed with practitioners, and expressed through the systems in which work happens. It should not become a separate approval process that delays contact with real problems.

I still believe that, but “governance must move at the pace of AI” needs one important qualification. A public service decision about rights, benefits, safety, or access should not move at the pace of a model release. Some decisions should be slow because their consequences are large and difficult to reverse.

> Governance must move at the speed of consequence.

A low-risk experiment inside a bounded environment might need a decision in hours. An internal workflow with limited and reversible effects might need days or weeks. A system that can affect a person’s rights, employment, safety, or access to a service needs stronger evidence and more careful review. The problem I described was not slowness itself. It was using one process and one clock for all three.

Governance that cannot distinguish a reversible experiment from an irreversible decision will block useful learning and still miss serious risk.

The same distinction applies to what became “legacy” after generative AI arrived. Legality, fairness, privacy, records, security, and accountability remain essential. Some of the operating rhythms around them became legacy almost overnight.

The second problem is judgement. By intuition, I do not mean instinct, confidence, or ability to sound certain in a meeting. I mean compressed experience: the pattern recognition that comes from building systems, watching them fail, testing what looked convincing, and changing your mind. It is the ability to recognise when a polished demo hides a brittle workflow, when an evaluation is measuring the wrong thing, and when a control belongs in infrastructure rather than a committee.

That judgement must also remain open to evidence, dissent, and correction. Otherwise intuition is only confidence in shiny clothes.

## The appointment metric

Australia now gives us a clear example of the difference between a role and a capability. The government requirement was narrower than some of the discussion around it.

Under the [APS AI Plan](https://www.govai.gov.au/aide/chief-ai-officers-who-are-they-and-why-they-matter), 106 non-corporate Commonwealth entities had to appoint a senior leader as Chief AI Officer by 30 June 2026. Corporate Commonwealth entities and Commonwealth companies were strongly encouraged to do the same. This was mainly an internal designation, not an order to recruit 106 external AI executives.

By 1 July, [Finance figures reported by the Australian Computer Society](https://ia.acs.org.au/article/2026/govt-agencies-that-missed-the-chief-ai-officer-deadline.html) showed that 104 of the 106 required entities had made the appointment. One of the two remaining entities had only come into existence that day. Across agencies and corporate entities, confirmed nominations had more than doubled from 56 on 15 June to 113 on 29 June.

In [*The Attacker Moves Second. So Did I.*](/blog/the-attacker-moves-second-so-did-i/), I described a KPI as a verifier with a bonus pool pointed at it. I left a footnote saying that the organisational version deserved a later post. Well, maybe this is it.

[Goodhart’s law](https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/mem2/papers/LHCE/goodhart.html) is usually summarised as: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Here, the real objective is safe, timely, and useful AI adoption. The observable proxy was whether an agency had named a Chief AI Officer. The target was appointment coverage by a fixed date. The intended response was to assign the role to an existing senior executive.

That response can be sensible. Existing leaders know the organisation, its mission, its authority, and the people who can make change happen. A deadline can also be a useful forcing function. It creates an owner where there may have been nobody clearly responsible before.

The risk appears when appointment coverage becomes a proxy for AI maturity. It measures administrative completion, not operational capacity. That is a limit of the metric, not a failure of the policy diagnosis.

## The policy got the diagnosis right

The appointment requirement and the Australian Government’s [Chief AI Officer information pack](https://www.finance.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-12/CAIO.pdf) came from the same APS AI Plan rollout. Dated 25 November 2025, the pack guided agencies as they selected existing senior leaders for the role. Read with the accountability and technical standards, it anticipated much of the operating problem.

The guidance says AI changes monthly, not annually. It warns about bureaucratic inertia and tells Chief AI Officers to challenge default assumptions and processes, and to work with early adopters and hands-on experts.

At the agency level, it also separates two jobs that are often merged in public discussion. The Chief AI Officer is meant to drive adoption, opportunity, and strategic change. The AI Accountable Official is accountable for implementing the government AI policy, including its governance and high-risk-use-case duties. The preference is for different people, although smaller agencies can combine the roles.

The wider policy adds another layer. Each in-scope AI use case must have an [Accountable Use Case Owner](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/accountability) who applies the impact-assessment, monitoring, evaluation, and revalidation requirements. Owners should also ensure that records demonstrate transparency and accountability. That accountability can be shared between business and technology roles. The [AI technical standard](https://www.digital.gov.au/policy/ai/AI-technical-standard/statement-3) identifies specialist roles for data, model development, integration, testing, ethics, compliance, and domain knowledge.

The applied layer is present, but it is not given the same agency-wide mandate.

Chief AI Officers and AI Accountable Officials are assigned to senior leaders. Applied roles are defined at team and use-case level. That difference can affect whether technical evidence reaches leaders in time and whether those leaders must respond.

One important part of that applied layer is harness engineering: the craft of configuring compound systems around AI models so they can perform valuable, complex work reliably, safely, and efficiently. As I argued in [*Where Capability Actually Lives in Agentic Engineering*](/blog/where-capability-actually-lives-in-agentic-engineering/), experts must drive this loop rather than appear only at fixed review points. They shape the task, tools, checks, and intervention points, then use evidence from real work to change the harness.

Harness engineering must enter alongside strategy and governance, before opportunity selection and risk framing are fixed. The tools, evaluations, permissions, observability, and human controls determine both what the system can do and what evidence governance can use. Without that applied knowledge, strategy chooses from imagined capability and governance controls imagined risks.

> Advice is not influence unless it can shape the decision.

The policy sees the pace problem and the need for practitioner input. It also provides for a central support function and peer network, so each officer is not expected to solve the problem alone. That is a strong design.

The implementation question remains. The appointment is an administrative fact. Capability is an operating system.

The question is whether practitioner support has standing influence when leaders decide.

## The leadership defence

The most obvious response to this argument is also correct: leaders do not need to be the best specialist in the room. Their job is to bring together people with different expertise, ask for advice, make trade-offs, and take responsibility for the decision.

But a leader can borrow expertise only when the expertise exists and can reach them. They cannot fully outsource the judgement needed to select the experts, frame the question, compare conflicting advice, and recognise weak evidence. They also need to know when the most confident person in the room is one model release behind.

The Chief AI Officer does not need to build a frontier model. They do need enough current understanding to avoid capture by a vendor, a control function, or a fashionable idea. They need to know what room to convene, what evidence to request, and when confidence has outrun the evidence.

Experience in machine learning, data, technology, policy, risk, or digital transformation can be invaluable. What matters is whether the candidate’s judgement is current, grounded in deployment, and open to revision. A frontier practitioner who cannot lead through an enterprise is no answer either.

The role is therefore not a search for one impossible person who contains every skill. It is the design of a leadership system that gives people with the necessary skills standing access to important decisions.

Commercial organisations have more freedom to design that system because they do not share an appointment deadline. Two recent Australian appointments show how different the answer can look. [Commonwealth Bank recruited Ranil Boteju](https://www.commbank.com.au/articles/newsroom/2025/11/ranil-boteju-chief-ai-officer.html) after more than 25 years across data, analytics, AI, and business transformation, including large-scale delivery at Lloyds. [Flight Centre appointed a 24-year internal veteran](https://www.itnews.com.au/news/flight-centre-to-embrace-ai-agents-ecommerce-consolidation-627489) whose path included software engineering, digital strategy, and a business-unit CIO role. One appointment brings external scale and cross-market experience. The other brings technical roots and deep knowledge of the organisation.

Neither résumé tells us whether the organisation has built the operating system around the appointment.

## Hire for the contradictions

The hiring process can be legacy too.

Role descriptions, competency sheets, panel composition, and interview routines are built from what an organisation already knows how to recognise. That is usually useful. It becomes dangerous when the role sits in a field whose boundaries, tools, and credible evidence are changing faster than the hiring process.

The wider AI labour market is under pressure. [PwC’s analysis of Australian job advertisements](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/issues/artificial-intelligence/job-barometer/aijb-2026-australia.pdf) found that postings requiring AI skills doubled from 2024 to 2025, reaching about 41,000. A [Robert Half-commissioned survey of 500 Australian hiring managers](https://www.roberthalf.com/content/dam/roberthalf/documents/au/en/indexed/press-release/Robert%20Half%20-%20AI%20roles%20-%20October%202025.pdf) found that employers cited scarce qualified candidates, competition, difficulty finding the right mix of technical and human skills, and difficulty assessing real AI capability in interviews. These are signals about AI hiring generally, not a count of qualified Chief AI Officer candidates.

The wrong response is to add more fashionable terms to the old scorecard.

Resist the familiar template. Look for paired tensions:

- depth and plasticity;
- conviction and willingness to update;
- strategic range and evidence from real deployments;
- speed and the ability to discriminate between risks;
- executive authority and protection of expert dissent;
- appetite for opportunity and willingness to stop weak work.

Interviews should test whether the candidate can hold both sides of a tension at once. Give them a realistic AI adoption case with incomplete facts. Ask for a decision and the evidence it requires. Then provide new information that should change the answer. See what they update, what they preserve, and what they reject. Ask which use case they would accelerate, which they would stop, who needs a voice, and what must be recorded.

This tests judgement, learning rate, and willingness to resist the answer the panel appears to reward. It does not test whether they can return the panel’s assumptions in this month’s AI vocabulary.

Ask for an example of evidence that changed their mind. Ask about a system they helped move from experiment to operation, what failed, and what they changed. Ask which governance controls they would put into the platform instead of the meeting. Ask whom they would hire first, and how they would know that person was better than them in the area that mattered.

Most of all, look for somebody willing to tell you something you do not initially agree with, then show you the evidence for it.

## Build the room

One of the things I think of as a personal “superpower” is finding and building rooms full of people much smarter than me, then keeping those rooms alive for years.

I spend time every day in active AI communities on Discord and X, and around open-source projects. These spaces make work visible: people build systems, test new models, publish evaluations, argue about failures, and update their views in public. That visibility is not proof, so claims still need to be tested against real systems. The people range from those building ambitious projects at home to researchers in frontier AI labs.

Most of the people I learn from in those communities are under thirty. Youth is not a qualification, and long experience remains valuable. But in a field changing this quickly, current expertise and organisational seniority do not always arrive together. Someone can have no executive title and still have years of concentrated experience with the systems an executive is now asked to govern.

This can be a blind spot in large organisations. They recognise capability most easily through senior titles, known firms, formal credentials, and polished case studies. Current practice often appears elsewhere: working systems, evaluations, failed experiments, and open-source work. Some of those practitioners already work inside the organisation, several layers from the decision. Others may never enter through a normal executive search.

Current technical practice is only one part of the room. Domain experts and people affected by a system must also shape consequential decisions.

Those practitioners do not need the executive seat. They need a paid, recurring role that gives them early access to real work and a protected route to challenge decisions. The accountable leader still decides. The difference is that current evidence can reach them before opportunity and risk framing are fixed.

A strong Chief AI Officer institutionalises that path. They find people with deeper current knowledge, test their claims, and make sure good evidence can change the organisation’s direction.

Ask the candidate: Whose expertise do you seek when yours runs out? What have they changed your mind about? How do you find people beyond your existing network? How will their expertise reach this organisation?

The appointments have created a named door in almost every agency. Now open it.
